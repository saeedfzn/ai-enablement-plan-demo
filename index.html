            3 · A prompt library for professional services

            Prompts people can copy and adapt, each with what to check before using the output and a data-sensitivity rating. Square brackets mark what you fill in. This is a starting set: in a real firm, each prompt would be tried on that team's own work by its champion and reviewed by Risk before release.

            Data-sensitivity ratings

            • ✓ Green Public information or your own generic wording. Fine in the approved tool.
            • ◷ Amber Internal firm information, or work material with the client and anything that would identify them taken out. Approved tool only, and only what the task needs. Swapping the name isn't always enough: if the detail would still point to one client, treat it as Red.
            • ⚠ Red Anything that identifies a client, and personal data beyond routine colleague names. Only in the approved tool, only where the engagement terms and firm policy allow it, and never in a free or personal AI account. If unsure, leave it out and ask Risk.

            4 · Responsible use in professional services

            The general principles I'd build every session around. The firm's own policy, its Risk team and each professional body's current guidance take precedence; training should point to them, not paraphrase them from memory.

            Clients

            Client confidentiality

            • The duty of confidentiality to clients doesn't change because the tool is new.
            • Engagement letters and client contracts can restrict or forbid AI tools. Check before you start.
            • Approved tools only, with the minimum client information the task needs.
            Legal

            Privilege

            • Legal privilege is narrower than many people assume, and may not cover advice from non-lawyers. It also depends on the material staying confidential, so where it goes matters.
            • Don't assume it applies to your work, and don't put privileged or dispute-related material into any tool without asking Legal or Risk first.
            Judgement

            Professional judgement stays human

            • AI can draft, summarise and suggest. It can't sign off, and it doesn't carry the responsibility. The person whose name is on the work does.
            • Apply the same professional scepticism to AI output as to any other source.
            Accuracy

            Check every output

            • Watch for invented figures, references, case names and quotes; out-of-date rates, thresholds and legislation; confident wording that hides a guess.
            • Check against the primary source, and keep a note of what you checked where the work needs it.
            People

            Personal data

            • UK GDPR and the Data Protection Act 2018 apply to personal data in prompts and documents, just as anywhere else.
            • Use the least personal data the task needs. Special category data, such as health information, needs extra care.
            • A new use of AI on personal data may need a data protection impact assessment. That is the data protection lead's call, so ask early.
            Oversight

            Regulators and transparency

            • Regulators and professional bodies are publishing guidance on AI. Broadly, they expect human accountability, quality control and good records.
            • I'd keep a short register of current guidance with Risk and update the training when it changes.
            • Tell clients, or record AI assistance on file, where policy or the engagement requires it.

            Would you? Four quick scenarios · open each one for the answer

            A manager wants a free AI app on their phone to summarise a client's draft accounts during the commute.

            No. Client financial information in an unapproved, personal tool breaks firm policy and risks a confidentiality breach, whatever the app's settings say. Use the approved tool on a firm device, if the engagement allows it.

            An associate asks the approved assistant to explain a new accounting standard in plain English, using only the published text.

            Yes, with checks. That's Green: public material. Check the explanation against the standard itself before relying on it, and don't quote the summary to a client as authoritative.

            A tax senior uses AI to draft a client letter and the letter quotes this year's allowance.

            Check before it goes. Rates and thresholds are a classic error. Check the figure against the official source, then the reviewer signs off as normal.

            Someone pasted a client email into a free chatbot yesterday, then realised.

            Report it now, without blame. Tell Risk or the service desk the same day. Fast reporting is what limits harm, and the training should say so plainly so people aren't tempted to hide it.
